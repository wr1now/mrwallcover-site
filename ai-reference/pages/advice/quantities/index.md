# How much wallpaper should you order?

Count the drops, not the square metres. Wallpaper is cut in full-height lengths, so the number of rolls follows the width of the wall, the height plus trim, the pattern repeat and the length of a roll. This guide shows the method by hand; our calculator cuts every drop from the rolls the way we do, shows you the plan, and adds one spare roll on its own line.

## Why width decides the number of drops

A drop is one full-height length of paper. Divide the wall width by the usable width of the roll and round up: that is the number of drops. Each drop is cut to the height to paper plus a trim of 5 cm at the top and 5 cm at the bottom, and a roll yields only as many whole drops as fit into its length. Round the drops up, the drops per roll down and the rolls up.

Take a simple wall: 5.00 metres wide and 2.70 metres high, with a free-match paper on a roll labelled 10.05 metres long and 0.53 metres wide. Each cut length is 2.80 metres. The width needs 10 drops: 5.00 ÷ 0.53 is 9.43, so nine full widths cover 4.77 metres and the tenth drop is a strip about 0.23 metres wide that still uses a full 2.80-metre length. Each roll yields 3 drops, because 10.05 ÷ 2.80 is 3.59 and the remainder is too short for a fourth. Ten drops at three per roll is 4 rolls.

Dividing the wall area by the roll area gives a different answer. The wall is 13.5 square metres and the roll about 5.33, so 13.5 ÷ 5.33 is 2.53, which rounds up to 3 rolls: one short, because the area method counts the offcuts and the narrow last strip as if they could be used. Enter the figures in the [calculator](https://www.mrwallcover.com/calculator/) and it gives 4, plus the spare. The example assumes a truly vertical, square wall, the usable dimensions on the label, a free match and no defects.

## How the pattern repeat changes the cut

A patterned paper has to be cut so the design lines up across the seam. With a straight match every drop starts at the same point in the pattern, so the next drop on a roll starts a whole number of repeats after the last one. Take a wall 230 cm wide and 265 cm high on a 55 cm by 10 m roll with a 65 cm repeat: each drop is 275 cm, the next starts 325 cm on, and we allow one full repeat at the start of every roll to find the pattern. Three drops fit, because 65 + 325 + 325 + 275 is 990 cm. Five drops need 2 rolls, and the spare makes 3. The last drop on a roll is cut at its own length, not the rounded one.

A half-drop moves every other drop down by half a repeat, and a third or quarter drop by a third or a quarter. The calculator works the cutting both ways, drop after drop along the roll, or all the drops of one phase together, and keeps the one that needs fewer rolls. The plan shows which drop comes from which roll. Large or irregular designs may also need the pattern to sit at a particular height, above a dado or centred on a chimney breast; that is set out on site, so the count is an order quantity, not a set-out.

## Why we count drops, not a percentage

A percentage added to the area, the usual 15–30%, can be too little for a large repeat and too much for a plain paper, and it hides the point where one more centimetre of height costs a whole roll. The calculator counts the drops instead and tells you when you are within a few centimetres of needing another roll. We still quote the allowance range in conversation, with staircases at the high end, around 30%, because of the angles.

Take the wall from the first example again: 13.5 square metres of wall on a roll of about 5.33 square metres. With a 15% allowance that is 2.91, so 3 rolls; with 30% it is 3.29, so 4 rolls. The drop count was 4, which is why the order follows the drops and the allowance is a check on it, not a substitute. Add the spare from the same batch on top.

## Do doors and windows save a drop?

Usually less than people hope. The drops either side of a window still run full height and are cut round it. Only the drops that sit wholly over a door or window become short pieces, above and below it, and we cut those from the offcuts of the rolls when the pattern lines up. Where the window has reveals, each side needs an extra part-drop to turn into the reveal, and the pieces above turn under into the head. Sometimes that saves a roll; often it does not.

## One batch, and one spare

Rolls from different production batches can differ slightly in colour, and that difference shows most at a seam. Order every roll, including the spare, from one batch, and check the batch number on every roll before anything is cut. Hang the first three drops, then stop and check the shade and the match in daylight. A spare roll from the same batch is also the only realistic way to make a later repair blend in.
