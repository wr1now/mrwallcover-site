How is a room kept clean and dry while it is papered? By getting the room right before the first roll is opened and keeping it that way until the paper has dried: walls dry in depth, the air within the maker's range, the rolls acclimatised, the dust contained where it is made, and everything that stays in the room covered. Whether the wall itself is sound, dry and true is covered in [wall preparation](/advice/wall-preparation/); this page is about the room around it.

## What temperature and humidity does a room need for hanging?

The range the maker gives for the product, and the ranges differ. Erfurt gives +18 to +25 °C at 30–60% relative humidity for its non-woven papers and Fromental 18–25 °C at 40–60% for its linen papers; Muraspec and Vescom, both contract makers, set a minimum instead.

| Maker and document | Temperature | Relative humidity | Also asks for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [Erfurt](https://www.erfurt.com/fileadmin/download/products/PRF_PVTM_Variovlies_M_120_2025_GB01.pdf), Variovlies data sheet | +18 to +25 °C | 30–60% | no draughts or strong heating while it dries |
| [Fromental](https://fromental.com/pages/fromental-shop-how-to-install-linen-and-specialist-fabric-wallpapers), linen and specialist fabric | 18–25 °C | 40–60% | heating and ventilation running normally three days before, during and after |
| [Farrow & Ball](https://www.farrow-ball.com/media/wysiwyg/Product-Advice-Sheets/Wallpapers/Crivelli_Rose_GBD30BT.pdf), product advice sheet | 10–25 °C | not high-humidity areas | ventilation in kitchens and bathrooms |
| [Muraspec](https://muraspec.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/Muraspec-UK-Hanging-Instructions-Feb-24.pdf), UK hanging instructions | site at least 12 °C | not given | good ventilation during hanging and drying |
| [Vescom](https://documents.vescom-cdn.com/f1873/EN%20-%20Algemeen%20verwerkingsvoorschrift%20-%20Vescom%20vinyl%2C%20olefin%20en%20textiel%20wandbekleding%202025.pdf), general specifications | wall at least 10 °C | not given | wall moisture content no more than 6% |

The instruction for the product on the wall is the one that counts, and where two products share a room, both have to be met.

## Why do the rolls wait in the room before they are hung?

So that paper and room are in the same condition before the first cut. Fromental's linen guide asks for the wallpaper to be stored at normal room temperature and humidity for at least three days before installation, in a weather-tight building with the heating and ventilation already running normally. An earlier Fromental guide for its bespoke silks ([archived copy](https://web.archive.org/web/20231130182143/https://fromental.co.uk/cdn/shop/files/Nonwoven_paper_backed_silk_Installation_Guide.pdf?v=17284818687869208217)) asked for the rolls to be kept in the room itself for at least three days, and for the room's conditions to stay the same before, during and for at least three days after the hang. Paper and textile move with the moisture in a room, as our [aftercare page](/aftercare/) explains. By Fromental's rule, a room heated for the first time on the morning of the hang is not ready for it.

## Why do sudden heat and draughts cause trouble?

Because a paper that dries too fast can open at the seams. Erfurt warns against rapid drying by draughts or strong heating, after which seams can open and show once the paper is painted, and equally against drying that is too slow, below 10 °C. [de Gournay](https://degournay.com/uploads/technical/document/2026_Hanging_Instructions-After-2025.pdf) adjusts for very dry rooms: in arid, low-humidity conditions it asks for the lining paper to be sized with paste so its panels dry more slowly. Our [problems and aftercare guide](/advice/wallcovering-problems-and-aftercare/) gives the household rule: steady heat, a ventilated room, and no fan heater pointed at a seam.

## Why is a damp wall not papered?

Because the makers rule it out, and moisture trapped behind a finish has nowhere good to go. [Sanderson](https://www.sanderson.design/pages/faqs) advises never hanging wallpaper over a damp wall, Fromental wants walls free from damp, moisture ingress or condensation, and Muraspec and Vescom want them dry in depth, not only dry to the touch. [Historic England](https://historicengland.org.uk/advice/technical-advice/flooding-and-historic-buildings/repair-and-emergency-works/repair/) notes that wet paper and paste can feed mould, and the government's [damp and mould guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/damp-and-mould-understanding-and-addressing-the-health-risks-for-rented-housing-providers/understanding-and-addressing-the-health-risks-of-damp-and-mould-in-the-home--2) that mould can be hidden on the back of wallpaper. A new finish over a damp wall hides the evidence while the problem carries on.

Most of these instructions give no figure for dry; Vescom asks for a building moisture content of no more than 6%. So a moisture reading decides, not the calendar or the feel of the surface, as our [wall preparation guide](/advice/wall-preparation/#is-it-dry) explains. A dry face can mislead: [Historic England](https://historicengland.org.uk/advice/technical-advice/flooding-and-historic-buildings/repair-and-emergency-works/drying/) warns that forced drying can leave the surface dry while the bulk of the wall is still wet, and for old buildings it asks for walls to be "dry for the purpose".

## How long does new plaster need to dry?

Until it is dry, which is not a fixed number of days. [British Gypsum](https://web.archive.org/web/20230626085127/https://www.british-gypsum.com/documents/product-data-sheet-pds/british-gypsum-pds-thistle-multifinish.pdf) says its Thistle plasters must be thoroughly dry before decorating, allows a coat of permeable paint in the meantime, and its data sheet gives no drying time in days. In the cold it asks for backgrounds to stay at 2 °C or above until dry and says to [allow for longer drying and setting times](https://web.archive.org/web/20240419061557/https://www.british-gypsum.com/technical-support/self-help-tools/faqs/what-minimum-temperature-gypsum-products). After a flood or a new damp-proof course, its advice for its [DriCoat](https://web.archive.org/web/20240119171459/https://www.british-gypsum.com/documents/product-data-sheet-pds/british-gypsum-pds-thistle-dricoat.pdf) undercoat is to delay replastering for as long as possible so the wall can dry out.

Dry plaster still needs preparing. [Cole & Son](https://cole-and-son.com/pages/hanging-instructions) says never to hang over new plaster or unprimed walls, and de Gournay that new plaster must be properly sized. Our [lining, primers and adhesives guide](/advice/lining-primers-adhesives/) covers sizing and priming.

## Will a dehumidifier fix damp?

No. A dehumidifier takes water out of the room's air; it does not stop water reaching the wall. A leak, a building defect or condensation has to be found and dealt with first. Historic England's advice for older homes is to [find and resolve the cause](https://historicengland.org.uk/advice/your-home/maintain-repair/decay/) of a problem, not just its symptoms, and the government's guidance says the underlying causes of damp and mould have to be tackled, because removing surface mould alone will not stop it coming back. Once the cause is fixed, a dehumidifier helps the room and then the wall dry out, and the moisture reading says when the wall has got there.

## When is a dehumidifier used, and which kind?

In damp areas we run a dehumidifier and an air purifier with a HEPA filter. Historic England, writing about drying old buildings after a flood, names the two kinds of dehumidifier, refrigerant and desiccant, asks for them to be controlled by humidistats with background heating of around 19–20 °C, and advises against aggressive dehumidifiers. [Meaco](https://blog.meaco.com/when-to-buy-a-desiccant-dehumidifier-and-when-to-buy-a-compressor-dehumidifier/), a UK maker of both, sets out the difference:

| What differs | Refrigerant (compressor) | Desiccant |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How it takes water out | air passes over a cold surface and the water condenses | a desiccant wheel absorbs it; an internal heater dries the wheel |
| Room it suits | above about 10 °C | below about 10 °C, such as a conservatory or a colder home |
| Air coming out | about 2 °C warmer than the room | about 10–12 °C warmer, enough to warm the room |

Below 10 °C, Meaco says, a compressor machine spends more and more of its time defrosting instead of drying. A desiccant's warm outflow is still heat on a drying paper, so place either machine with the wall in mind and never aim it at fresh seams. Historic England adds that heaters used without enough ventilation can raise the absolute humidity rather than lower it. A humidistat holds the room at a set humidity; for the hang, the target is the maker's range above.

## What does a HEPA air purifier do, and what does it not do?

It cleans the air that passes through it. HEPA means high-efficiency particulate air, and HEPA filters are classed under BS EN 1822-1 or ISO 29463-1. [NHS England](https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/application-of-hepa-filter-devices-for-air-cleaning-in-healthcare-spaces-guidance-and-standards/) gives at least 99.95% for class H13 and 99.995% for H14, at the particle size a filter finds hardest, around 0.3 µm, and adds that a filter fitted in a device sometimes performs below its class. The [Department for Education](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/69832b1ba1758c98acc243cf/Air_cleaning_unit_specification_-_Department_for_Education.pdf)'s specification for air cleaning units in education and childcare settings asks for H13 or better. The [US EPA](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/what-hepa-filter) uses a US Department of Energy definition instead: at least 99.97% of particles of 0.3 µm. The air purifier we run in damp areas has a HEPA filter, and the DfE's [ventilation guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ventilation-and-air-quality-in-education-and-childcare-settings/ventilation-and-air-quality-in-education-and-childcare-settings) says such units can help to reduce airborne contaminants, fungal spores among them.

It does not fix damp: the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/guide-air-cleaners-home) says portable air cleaners do not address the cause of mould, which is a water or moisture problem. It does not replace fresh air either: the DfE and [UKHSA](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/ventilation-to-reduce-the-spread-of-respiratory-infections-including-covid-19) both say air cleaning is not a substitute for ventilation, and UKHSA lists the size of the area among the things to consider when choosing a unit. A particle filter does not take out gases, smells included: the EPA says gases need an activated carbon filter or another filter made for them. Dust that has settled on surfaces is out of its reach unless disturbed, the EPA notes.

## What are ZipWall temporary walls?

Floor-to-ceiling dust screens held up by spring-loaded poles. We put up ZipWall temporary walls to contain dust. [ZipWall](https://www.zipwall.com/about/), the American company that makes the system, describes telescopic poles that hold plastic sheeting, or its reusable fabric panels, against the ceiling. Its [UK site](https://www.zipwall.eu/faqs/) gives working heights up to 3.12 m, 3.6 m or 6.1 m depending on the pole, with poles 3–3.7 m apart, closer where the air moves. ZipWall describes the pole head as non-skid and non-marking, made to hold the sheet [without damaging the ceiling](https://www.zipwall.com/product/zipwall-12-2-pack/).

Access is through zips stuck to the sheet, a ZipDoor kit taped to a door frame, or a magnetic door that closes itself, which [ZipWall says](https://www.zipwall.com/faqs/) keeps the barrier sealed. FoamRail seals press the sheet to ceiling, walls and floor without tape, and a sticky mat at the door is made to stop dust being tracked out. ZipWall advises testing its double-sided tape somewhere inconspicuous first, because paint finishes vary. These descriptions are ZipWall's own.

## How is sanding dust kept in one room or corridor?

By enclosing it, taking it at the tool and cleaning up without raising it again. [HSE's construction dust guidance](https://www.hse.gov.uk/pubns/cis36.pdf) lists enclosing the work with sheeting or temporary screens among its controls, with extraction through a unit of the right class, H, M or L, not a general commercial vacuum. HSE adds that [a HEPA filter in a general commercial vacuum is not enough](https://www.hse.gov.uk/pubns/cis69.pdf), and advises against [dry sweeping](https://www.hse.gov.uk/construction/healthrisks/hazardous-substances/sweeping-cleaning.htm), pointing to an H or M class vacuum for fine dust. Where preparation is in our scope, sanding is done with dust extraction, as our [FAQ](/faq/#preparation) says. A barrier across a doorway or a length of corridor, both uses ZipWall lists, keeps the rest of the floor on the clean side.

## Is the sheeting flame-retardant?

Not necessarily: it depends on the sheet. ZipWall describes its [Commercial ZipDoor](https://www.zipwall.com/product/zipdoor-commercial-door-kit/) as "4 mil flame retardant plastic sheeting" and its standard ZipDoor as "not flame retardant", sells its own 3-mil dust-barrier sheeting as not flame-retardant, and says its poles take sheeting from 1 mil to about 10 mil, "including many fire-rated and reinforced plastics". Its FAQs and door pages name no fire test standard; the one rating its UK site gives is a US building-code class for its [flame-retardant fabric panels](https://www.zipwall.eu/zipfast-panels-flame-retardant/).

On UK sites, [HSE](https://www.hse.gov.uk/pubns/priced/hsg168.pdf) advises flexible protective coverings that are flame-retardant to LPS 1207 or an equivalent such as Certifire TS63, marked with their certification, and the Fire Protection Association's [Joint Code of Practice](https://www.thefpa.co.uk/resource-download/858) requires them where it applies. The certification belongs to the sheeting, not to the poles: [Visqueen](https://visqueen.com/datasheet/13871712/)'s flame-retardant polythene, for example, is certified to LPS 1207 for screening and covering surfaces indoors. Both say at least one fire escape stair should be kept free of protective coverings, because flame-retardant material can still burn, and the Joint Code asks every party to check with its insurer on each project.

## How are floors, furniture and finished rooms protected?

As part of the hang, not as an extra: protection of floors, furniture and finished joinery belongs in the scope, and in an occupied home or a working hotel the work has to be contained and tidied daily, as our [cost guide](/advice/installation-cost/#what-about-access-protection-and-the-awkward-parts) says; at [Pendley Manor](/projects/pendley-manor-hotel/) the floors were protected throughout. HSE describes protective coverings as a common feature of fit-out wherever finished doors, handrails, floor coverings and panels need protecting, and warns that coverings and the tape that holds them can add substantially to the fire load, hence its advice on flame-retardant ones.

## Can the work go ahead while the house or hotel is occupied?

Yes. Most of the hotel work on our record was done in buildings that stayed open. At the [DoubleTree by Hilton London – West End](/projects/doubletree-west-end/), levels 5 and 6, 30 rooms and their corridors, were completed in July 2019 while the hotel stayed open, with guests on the other floors. At [Brown's Hotel](/projects/browns-hotel-mayfair/), open throughout, access, timing, protection and housekeeping were worked out with the main contractor and the hotel's management, and the work was kept contained and quiet.

Dust stays on the work side behind a barrier sealed at ceiling, walls and floor, with a door that closes behind each person and extraction at the tool. Smell needs a different answer, because it is a gas: a HEPA filter alone will not take it out of a corridor. For gases the EPA points to an activated carbon filter, and it names removing the source and ventilating as the most effective ways to improve indoor air. HSE asks for a partly occupied building's emergency plan to cover the people outside the work area. The [hotel specification guide](/advice/hotel-wallcovering-specification/#how-is-the-work-phased-around-guests) covers working hours, room release and the daily handover.

## When does this advice stop being reliable?

This is general guidance for homes and hotels in reasonable condition. Where mould covers more than a small patch, the government's guidance says larger areas should only be dealt with by qualified professionals, and that removing wallpaper can release a significant amount of spores. After a flood, Historic England advises removing and discarding non-historic wallcoverings, consulting a specialist conservator about historic ones, and not force-drying traditional buildings. In buildings built or refurbished before 2000, [HSE](https://www.hse.gov.uk/asbestos/diagrams.htm) says asbestos can be present, with textured decorative coatings on walls and ceilings among the places it lists; anyone who finds material that may contain it should [stop work](https://www.hse.gov.uk/asbestos/workers.htm) and ask their employer or the building owner whether there is asbestos in the building, and some asbestos work may only be done by a licensed contractor. Healthcare buildings follow their own infection-control guidance. The maker's instruction for the actual product always takes precedence over this page.
