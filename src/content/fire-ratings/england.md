For buildings other than dwellings in England, the starting point is [Approved Document B, Volume 2](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/fire-safety-approved-document-b). Blocks of flats, including their common corridors and stairs, are covered by Volume 1 instead; its Table 4.1 also sets B-s3,d2 for the common areas of blocks of flats, with the same BS EN 15102 wallcovering note, and C-s3,d2 for circulation spaces within a dwelling. It gives guidance on meeting **requirement B2** of the Building Regulations 2010, which is the legal requirement. B2 says internal linings must adequately resist the spread of flame over their surfaces and, if ignited, have a rate of heat release or fire growth that is reasonable in the circumstances.

Three things follow from how the document is built:

- **The law is the requirement; the Approved Document is guidance.** Following the guidance is one way to show the requirement is met, but it does not guarantee compliance in every case. Under section 7 of the Building Act 1984, not following an approved document does not by itself make anyone liable, but it can be relied on as tending to show a breach, and following it as tending to show compliance. A designer can meet B2 another way, for example through a fire-engineered strategy agreed with the building-control body, which decides whether it is satisfied.
- **Section 6 and Table 6.1 set minimum classes by location.** The table is short and the categories matter: small rooms (up to 4 m² in residential accommodation, up to 30 m² in non-residential accommodation), other rooms, and other circulation spaces. A hotel bedroom, a meeting room, a corridor and a protected stair are not the same location.
- **The table's own note matters for wallcoverings.** Note 1 to Table 6.1, on the circulation-space row, says that wallcoverings conforming to BS EN 15102, achieving at least class C-s3,d2 and bonded to a class A2-s3,d2 substrate, will also be acceptable. That is a route that depends on both the product and what it is bonded to. It is not a rule that any class C wallcovering will do in a corridor.

Read these alongside the table:

- **Paragraph 6.4.** Parts of walls in rooms may be of lower performance than the table, but no worse than D-s3,d2, and the total area is limited (less than half the room's floor area, up to 20 m² in residential and 60 m² in non-residential accommodation). This applies to rooms, not circulation spaces.
- **What counts as a wall or a ceiling.** Paragraphs 6.2 to 6.6 define them. Glazing and steep ceilings count as wall; doors, frames, skirtings, architraves and fitted furniture do not.
- **Other sections.** Paragraph 6.12 points to the guidance for escape stairs, firefighting shafts and surfaces above fire-protecting suspended ceilings. The project's fire strategy or the client's brief may also set higher classes than the table.
- **Smoke and droplets.** The table's classes all carry s3 and d2, the least demanding subclasses. B2's guidance does not address the generation of smoke and fumes. A project can still ask for s1 or d0, so check the specification rather than assuming the table is the whole requirement.
- **Appendix B.** Test evidence must suit the intended use. Small differences such as the fixing method, joints, air gaps or insulation can significantly affect performance and should be tested or assessed, an assessment following the relevant extended-application standard or the principles of BS EN 15725 and being carried out by an organisation with the necessary expertise; and a reaction-to-fire classification is valid only within its direct field of application: on a substrate of the class it was tested on, with a density of at least 75 per cent of the standard substrate's. Paragraph B9 adds that standard calcium silicate board is not representative of gypsum plasterboard, because of the paper layer, though it represents most gypsum plasters over 650 kg/m³, and that a classification obtained on plasterboard is also acceptable on gypsum plaster. Much of the product evidence below was obtained on calcium silicate or fibre-cement board.

Who resolves applicability: for building work, the designer or fire engineer for the project, with the building-control body that will approve the work. If that work makes a significant change to a building in use, the responsible person must also review the fire risk assessment (Fire Safety Order, article 9(3)). For redecorating a building in use that is not building work, there is no building-control approval: the responsible person decides, through the fire risk assessment (see below).
