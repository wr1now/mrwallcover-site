For buildings other than dwellings in England, the starting point is [Approved Document B, Volume 2](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/fire-safety-approved-document-b). It gives guidance on meeting **requirement B2** of the Building Regulations 2010, which is the legal requirement. B2 says internal linings must adequately resist the spread of flame over their surfaces and, if ignited, have a rate of heat release or fire growth that is reasonable in the circumstances.

Three things follow from how the document is built:

- **The law is the requirement; the Approved Document is guidance.** Following the guidance is one way to show the requirement is met. A designer can meet B2 another way, for example through a fire-engineered strategy, and building control decides whether it is satisfied.
- **Section 6 and Table 6.1 set minimum classes by location.** The table is short and the categories matter: small rooms (up to 4 m² in residential accommodation, up to 30 m² in non-residential accommodation), other rooms, and other circulation spaces. A hotel bedroom, a meeting room, a corridor and a protected stair are not the same location.
- **The table's own note matters for wallcoverings.** Note 1 to Table 6.1, on the circulation-space row, says that wallcoverings conforming to BS EN 15102, achieving at least class C-s3,d2 and bonded to a class A2-s3,d2 substrate, will also be acceptable. That is a route that depends on both the product and what it is bonded to. It is not a rule that any class C wallcovering will do in a corridor.

Read these alongside the table:

- **Paragraph 6.4.** Parts of walls in rooms may be of lower performance than the table, but no worse than D-s3,d2, and the total area is limited (less than half the room's floor area, up to 20 m² in residential and 60 m² in non-residential accommodation). This applies to rooms, not circulation spaces.
- **What counts as a wall or a ceiling.** Paragraphs 6.2 to 6.6 define them. Glazing and steep ceilings count as wall; doors, frames, skirtings, architraves and fitted furniture do not.
- **Other sections.** Paragraph 6.12 points to the guidance for escape stairs, firefighting shafts and surfaces above fire-protecting suspended ceilings. The project's fire strategy or the client's brief may also set higher classes than the table.
- **Smoke and droplets.** The table's classes all carry s3 and d2, the least demanding subclasses. B2's guidance does not address the generation of smoke and fumes. A project can still ask for s1 or d0, so check the specification rather than assuming the table is the whole requirement.
- **Appendix B.** Test evidence must suit the intended use. Small differences such as the fixing method, joints, air gaps or insulation can significantly affect performance, and a reaction-to-fire classification is valid only within its direct field of application: on a substrate of the class it was tested on, with a density of at least 75 per cent of the standard substrate's.

Who resolves applicability: the designer or fire engineer for the project, with the building-control body that will approve the work. For an existing building in use, the responsible person's fire risk assessment also has a say (see below).
