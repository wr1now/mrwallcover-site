The lining classes in building-regulations guidance apply to **building work**: a new building, an extension, a material change of use, or work to an existing building that needs to meet the regulations. Settle before work starts whether a refurbishment counts as building work and, if it does, which edition of the guidance applies. The designer, if there is one, or a building-control body can advise. The answer decides who approves the linings: the building-control route, or the responsible person. Approved Document B lists installers among those responsible for building work, so we raise the question rather than assume the answer.

**Buildings in use.** Fire-safety law for buildings in use applies to most premises other than private homes. On this page, "a building in use" means one that this law covers. In England and Wales that is the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005, which requires the "responsible person" to carry out and keep under review a suitable and sufficient fire risk assessment and to take general fire precautions. It does not apply inside a private dwelling (article 6(1)(a)). In a building with two or more homes it does apply to the common parts, such as shared halls, corridors and stairs. The [Fire Safety Act 2021](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2021/24/section/1) confirmed that the Order also covers the structure, the external walls and the doors between each flat and the common parts (article 6(1A)). That change came into force in Wales on 1 October 2021 and in England on 16 May 2022.

Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own law: Part 3 of the [Fire (Scotland) Act 2005](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/asp/2005/5/section/78) and Part 3 of the [Fire and Rescue Services (Northern Ireland) Order 2006](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/nisi/2006/1254/article/50). Both require an assessment of fire risks and fire-safety measures, and neither applies to domestic premises. In both, the stairs, passages and other parts shared by the occupants of more than one home count as domestic premises (section 78(4); article 50(6)). So the common parts of a block of flats are outside the main duties there. Only a narrow duty to maintain facilities for firefighters applies to them (regulation 24 of the Fire Safety (Scotland) Regulations 2006 and of the Fire Safety Regulations (Northern Ireland) 2010). The duties do apply to houses in multiple occupation that need a licence in Scotland, and to all houses in multiple occupation in Northern Ireland.

**When redecorating is not building work.** In England and Wales, redecorating on its own is not normally building work: the linings requirement (B2) is not one of the requirements that make an alteration "material" ([Building Regulations 2010, regulation 3](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2010/2214/regulation/3)). Where the job is not building work, no building-control body approves it. The responsible person decides, through the fire risk assessment, often with a fire risk assessor. Inside a private home these duties do not apply, so there is no responsible person. Approved Document B warns that guidance for assessing existing buildings is often less demanding than guidance for new buildings, so it is unlikely to suit building work. See England's Volume 2, paragraph 0.9, and the Welsh Volume 2, paragraph 0.19. Where the job is building work, such as an extension, a material change of use or a fit-out that counts as building work, the classes come from the building-regulations guidance or the project's fire strategy.

In Scotland, replacing a wall lining or covering with one of the same general type needs no building warrant, so no verifier checks it. The work must still leave the standard no worse than before ([Building (Scotland) Regulations 2004](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ssi/2004/406/schedule/3), regulation 5 and schedule 3, type 25). The owner or client has that duty. Other lining work in a building open to the public, such as a hotel, may need a warrant. In Northern Ireland, "building work" includes a "structural alteration": any work the regulations would control in a new building. Internal linings are controlled there ([Building Regulations (Northern Ireland) 2012](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/nisr/2012/192/regulation/34), regulations 2 and 34), so ask the district council's building control whether a wallcovering job needs an application before assuming it does not.

The official risk-assessment guides for England and Wales discuss wall and ceiling linings as part of that assessment. The guide for hotels and other sleeping accommodation says care is needed with multi-layer decorative systems such as wallpapers and oil-based paints; the offices-and-shops guide lists linings by national class and notes that additional finishes may harm a surface's fire performance. Both are older documents and use the national class names alongside a note that the European equivalent is acceptable.

None of the official sources we checked sets a universal requirement to strip out and replace existing wallcoverings because the classification system has changed. What they do require is an assessment of the actual building. When a hotel or office is being refurbished, the new wallcovering's evidence, the existing layers underneath and the location of each wall all belong in that assessment.
