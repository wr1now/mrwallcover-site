The lining classes in building-regulations guidance apply to **building work**: a new building, an extension, a material change of use, or work to an existing building that needs to meet the regulations. Whether a particular refurbishment counts as building work, and which edition of the guidance applies to it, should be settled with the designer and the building-control body before work starts. Approved Document B lists installers among those responsible for building work, so we raise the question rather than assume the answer.

**Occupied premises** are covered by fire-safety law for buildings in use. In England and Wales that is the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005, which requires the "responsible person" to carry out and keep under review a suitable and sufficient fire risk assessment and to take general fire precautions. Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own fire-safety legislation. The official risk-assessment guides for England and Wales discuss wall and ceiling linings as part of that assessment. The guide for hotels and other sleeping accommodation says care is needed with multi-layer decorative systems such as wallpapers and oil-based paints; the offices-and-shops guide lists linings by national class and notes that additional finishes may harm a surface's fire performance. Both are older documents and use the national class names alongside a note that the European equivalent is acceptable.

None of the official sources we checked sets a universal requirement to strip out and replace existing wallcoverings because the classification system has changed. What they do require is an assessment of the actual building. When a hotel or office is being refurbished, the new wallcovering's evidence, the existing layers underneath and the location of each wall all belong in that assessment.
